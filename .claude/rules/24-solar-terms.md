# The 24 Solar Terms

> Twenty-four seasonal markers spaced at every 15° of the Sun's
> tropical ecliptic longitude. The Han-dynasty canonical division of
> the solar year. Twelve **sectional** terms alternating with twelve
> **mid** terms. Four of them are cardinal — equinoxes and solstices —
> and those four align with four of the six Damanhurian Great Rites.

---

## What they are

The **24 Solar Terms** (**二十四节气** *èrshísì jiéqì*) divide the Sun's
apparent annual path around the ecliptic into 24 equal arcs of **15°**
each. A term begins the moment the Sun's tropical longitude crosses
the boundary into the next 15° sector. The year cycles from 0° (Spring
Equinox, **Chūnfēn**) through 360° back to 0°, ticking off one term
roughly every **15.2 days**.

The system is the canonical agricultural calendar of East Asia.
Codified in the Han dynasty (the *Huáinánzǐ*, 139 BCE, contains an
early systematic list; the *Tàichū* reform of 104 BCE locked the
sequence used today). Inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of
the **Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity** in 2016.

[SOURCE: TCM / Chinese astronomical tradition]

The 24 terms are not "Chinese zodiac" (that is the 12-animal
duodenary). They are not the **24 hours** (那是時刻, *shíkè*) of the
day. They are 24 *seasonal* positions of the Sun across the **year**.

## The sectional/mid alternation

The 24 terms alternate in two interleaved series of 12:

- **Sectional terms** (**节** *jié*, "nodes") fall at the **odd-numbered**
  positions in the 0°-based enumeration (0°, 30°, 60°, … 330°). Twelve
  total. These are the boundary markers between solar months.
- **Mid terms** (**气** *qì*, "breaths") fall at the **even-numbered**
  positions (15°, 45°, 75°, … 345°). Twelve total. These sit at the
  midpoint of each solar month.

The pairing *jié-qì* (节气, "node-breath") gives the system its
collective name. Every solar month has exactly one sectional term at
its beginning and one mid term at its midpoint. The mid term is what
the traditional Chinese lunar calendar uses to **anchor the lunisolar
month numbering**: the month containing the *Yǔshuǐ* mid term (Rain
Water, 330°) is Lunar Month 1, and so on.

[MATHEMATICAL FACT — the 15° / 30° alternation is geometric, not
seasonal-empirical.]

## The tropical framing

The terms are anchored to the **tropical** ecliptic, not the sidereal
one. Term 1 (**Lìchūn**, Beginning of Spring) is fixed at solar
longitude 315° measured from the vernal equinox of the current epoch.
The cardinal terms (Chūnfēn, Xiàzhì, Qiūfēn, Dōngzhì) are *defined* as
the equinoxes and solstices themselves — they cannot drift against
the seasons because they *are* the seasons' astronomical definition.

This means: **do not apply Lahiri ayanamsa** or any other sidereal
correction here. The Indian *nakṣatra* system uses sidereal longitude
and drifts by ~50″ of arc per year against the seasons; the Chinese
solar terms do not, because they are referenced to the equinox itself.

Drift over centuries is therefore **negligible at the seasonal scale**.
What does drift slightly is the *Gregorian date* on which each term
falls: the Gregorian calendar approximates the tropical year well but
not perfectly, so the dates wobble by **±1 day** from year to year
(occasionally ±2 across long stretches). Lìchūn falls on Feb 3 in some
years and Feb 4 in others; this is a calendrical artifact, not a drift
in the term itself.

[MATHEMATICAL FACT — the wobble is the residual between the Gregorian
mean year (365.2425 d) and the tropical year (365.24219 d), plus
leap-year quantization.]

## The four cardinal terms (and the Damanhurian Great Rites)

The four cardinal terms are the equinoxes and solstices themselves —
the astronomical moments at which the Sun crosses 0°, 90°, 180°, and
270° tropical longitude. Four of the **six Damanhurian Great Rites**
anchor to these same astronomical events. This is the load-bearing
cross-tradition link between the Chinese seasonal canon and the
Damanhurian ritual year.

[ANALYTICAL CONTRIBUTION — the alignment itself is the observation
that the same astronomical event carries weight in both traditions.
The Chinese sources do not invoke the Damanhurian rites; the
Damanhurian sources do not invoke the *jié-qì* system. The shared
anchor is the celestial geometry. The Astrolabium reports both layers
when an equinox or solstice term is current.]

### 春分 Chūnfēn — Spring Equinox (0°)

The Sun crosses the celestial equator ascending. Day and night
balanced. In the Damanhurian calendar this is the **Great Rite of the
Spring Equinox**, anchored to Month 7 (**OSIRIS** — The Resurrected).
The system's pivot from the Albedo movement's interior work into its
public celebration. Both traditions treat Chūnfēn / Spring Equinox as
the moment yang has fully returned and the year's overt action begins.

### 夏至 Xiàzhì — Summer Solstice (90°)

The Sun reaches its highest declination. Longest day, shortest night.
**Great Rite of the Summer Solstice**, anchored to Month 10 (**SET**
— The Destroyer). The yang maximum that, the moment after it is
reached, begins to decline — what the *Yìjīng* tradition calls the
hexagram of *Gòu* (44, **姤**, "the encounter" — one yin line returning
beneath five yang). Both systems mark this as the peak that is also
the turning.

### 秋分 Qiūfēn — Autumn Equinox (180°)

The Sun crosses the celestial equator descending. Day and night
balanced again. **Great Rite of the Autumn Equinox**, anchored to
Month 1 (**ISIS** — The Gatherer). This is the **start of the Divine
Year** in the Astrolabium's calendar (Year 1 = NM nearest the Autumn
Equinox of 1949). Qiūfēn is therefore not just a seasonal marker but
the calendrical hinge of the entire Astrolabium system.

### 冬至 Dōngzhì — Winter Solstice (270°)

The Sun reaches its lowest declination. Longest night, shortest day.
**Great Rite of the Winter Solstice**, anchored to Month 4 (**EOROS**
— The Director / Horus). The yin maximum that contains the first
returning yang — *Fù* (24, **復**, "return" — one yang line returning
beneath five yin). In Chinese folk tradition Dōngzhì is a major
**festival** (冬至节 *Dōngzhì jié*), one of the year's eight ritual
high points: families gather, eat dumplings or tāngyuán, and observe
the return of light. Both traditions read this as the deepest point
that is already the recovery.

[SOURCE: TCM (Chinese seasonal canon) + SOURCE: Damanhurian (Great
Rites) + ANALYTICAL CONTRIBUTION (the alignment claim).]

The two Damanhurian Great Rites that do **not** align with cardinal
solar terms are the **Day of the Dead** (early November, traditional —
nearest term is 立冬 Lìdōng at 225°) and the **Divine Marriage**
(May 24, lineage anniversary — nearest term is 小满 Xiǎomǎn at 60°).
These are anchored to the Damanhurian calendar's own rhythms, not to
solar longitude per se.

## How the engine computes the current term

```python
from astrolabium.engine import solar_terms

state = solar_terms.get_current_solar_term(dt)
# returns:
#   {
#     "index": 7,                 # 1..24
#     "chinese": "立夏",
#     "pinyin": "Lìxià",
#     "english": "Beginning of Summer",
#     "longitude_deg": 45,
#     "type": "sectional",
#     "started_at": <datetime>,
#     "next_term": {...},
#     "next_transition_at": <datetime>
#   }
```

The computation chain:

1. `ephem.Sun(dt)` gives apparent geocentric position.
2. Convert to **tropical** ecliptic longitude (referenced to the
   equinox of date, not J2000.0 and not sidereal).
3. The current term is `floor((longitude_deg - 315) mod 360 / 15) + 1`
   — the offset by 315° puts Lìchūn at index 1 as the canonical year
   start.
4. The term metadata is looked up from
   `astrolabium/data/solar_terms.json`.

[MATHEMATICAL FACT — this is deterministic given the ephemeris.]

## How the engine finds the next transition

The next term boundary is a moment when the Sun's tropical longitude
crosses an exact multiple of 15°. The engine uses a **two-stage**
search:

1. **Coarse scan.** From the input `dt`, step forward in 1-hour
   intervals, computing the Sun's longitude at each step. Stop when
   the longitude crosses the next 15° boundary. This bounds the
   transition to a 1-hour window.
2. **Bisection.** Within that hour, bisect to the target precision
   (typically 1 second). At each midpoint, recompute the Sun's
   longitude via ephem and compare to the target boundary.

The Sun moves through the ecliptic at ~0.9856°/day or ~0.041°/hour, so
a 1-hour coarse scan never skips a 15° boundary. Bisection converges
in ~12 iterations to second-level precision.

[MATHEMATICAL FACT — the scan + bisection is exact within
ephem's intrinsic precision, which is sub-arcsecond.]

## Saga Dawa overlap

Saga Dawa (ས་ག་ཟླ་བ་, *Sa ga zla ba*), the fourth Tibetan lunar
month, commemorates the birth, enlightenment, and parinirvāṇa of the
Buddha. Its full moon (Saga Dawa Düchen) is the year's most
merit-multiplying day in the Tibetan Buddhist calendar. The lunar
month typically spans late April through late June in any given
Gregorian year, which means it sits across the solar terms **Lìxià
→ Xiǎomǎn → Mángzhòng** (terms 7, 8, 9 — longitudes 45° through 75°).

The Chinese reading of this window — "**yang energy rises, all life
begins to flourish**" — is the canonical seasonal interpretation of
Lìxià and the immediately following terms. The two traditions are
reading the same astronomical window: the period when northern-hemisphere
sunlight has returned strongly, ground temperatures have stabilized
above freezing, and the biosphere's metabolic activity inflects upward.

The Tibetan tradition reads this as the appropriate season for the
Buddha's central biographical events; the Chinese tradition reads it
as the season when yang surges into manifest form. Both place ritual
weight on the same astronomical interval. The Astrolabium can report
this overlap when a user queries during the May–June window.

[SOURCE: Tibetan Buddhist calendar / SOURCE: TCM / ANALYTICAL
CONTRIBUTION for the overlap observation.]

## Chinese festivals tied to solar terms

A handful of the 24 terms carry not just seasonal-marker weight but
**festival** weight in the Chinese folk calendar. The two that matter
most for cross-cultural reporting:

**清明节 Qīngmíng jié — Tomb-Sweeping Festival.** Coincides with term 5,
**Qīngmíng** (Pure Brightness, longitude 15°, approximately April 4–5).
Families visit ancestral graves, sweep them, offer food and joss. The
festival's spring timing relates directly to the term's name —
*qīngmíng* means "pure and bright," the season when the air clears
after winter and the ancestral relationship can be tended in the
visible world. One of the few traditional Chinese festivals that
anchors to a **solar** term rather than a **lunar** date.

**冬至节 Dōngzhì jié — Winter Solstice Festival.** Coincides with term
22, **Dōngzhì** (Winter Solstice, longitude 270°, approximately Dec
21–22). Family gathering, ritual meal (dumplings in the north,
tāngyuán in the south), the observance that the year has reached its
darkest point and the return of light has begun. In some periods of
Chinese history Dōngzhì was a more important festival than Lunar New
Year itself.

The other 22 terms are agricultural markers without strong festival
practice. Some have regional folk customs (Lìchūn's "biting spring"
*咬春* radish-eating, Mángzhòng's rice-planting observances) but
nothing on the scale of Qīngmíng or Dōngzhì.

[SOURCE: Chinese folk tradition.]

## What never to do

- **Never confuse the 24 Solar Terms with the 24 hours.** They are
  completely different units — one is a yearly seasonal division at
  every 15° of solar longitude, the other is a daily clock division.
  Same number, no relation.
- **Never apply Lahiri ayanamsa or any sidereal correction here.** The
  system is tropical. The cardinal terms *are* the equinoxes and
  solstices; correcting them away from those events destroys the
  system.
- **Never treat the dates as Gregorian-fixed.** Each term wobbles by
  ±1 day from year to year (occasionally ±2). The Gregorian calendar
  approximates the tropical year well but not exactly. The engine
  computes the actual transition moment via ephem; reading off a
  fixed-date table is wrong in every year that wobbles.
- **Never present the Damanhurian Great Rite alignment as a
  [SOURCE: TCM] claim.** The four-cardinal alignment is
  [ANALYTICAL CONTRIBUTION]. The Chinese sources do not name the
  Damanhurian rites, and the Damanhurian sources do not name the
  *jié-qì*. The two traditions converge on the same astronomical
  events independently; the convergence is observable, the
  identification is analytical.
- **Never report a solar term without an ephemeris computation.**
  Tabulated dates are a teaching reference, not the operational
  source.
- **Never call mid terms "minor" or sectional terms "major."** They
  are not a hierarchy — they are an alternation. The "sectional /
  mid" distinction is structural (where they fall in each solar
  month), not evaluative.
